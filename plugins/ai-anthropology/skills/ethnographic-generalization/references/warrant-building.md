# Building the Warrant

A generalizing claim earns its reach through four operations: translating
emic categories into etic constructs, hunting disconfirmation, naming
rivals, and disciplining comparison. Each has a gate where the researcher
decides; the machine assembles, proposes, and keeps the record. The chain of
evidence that results is what makes the claim auditable.

## Emic to Etic: First-Order and Second-Order Constructs

The claim will be written in analytical language; the data speak in
participants' language. The bridge is the distinction between first-order
constructs — the common-sense categories, vernacular explanations, and folk
taxonomies participants use to make sense of their lives — and second-order
constructs — the analytical abstractions the researcher formulates to
interpret those first-order meanings in relation to disciplinary debates
(Schütz's distinction, standard in ethnographic methodology). Analytical
generalization happens precisely at this translation point, and both
failure poles are common:

- **Radical reductionism.** The etic framework imposed so aggressively that
  participant meanings are overwritten — the theory would have found itself
  in any data.
- **Uncritical mimicry.** First-order accounts reproduced without
  analytical elevation — vivid, faithful, and unable to speak to anything
  beyond themselves.

Work the translation as one table, presented for one confirm-or-revise
decision per round:

| Emic category (participants' term) | What it does for participants | Candidate etic construct | What the translation preserves | What it loses |
|---|---|---|---|---|

The last column is not optional. Every translation loses something, and a
researcher who can name what theirs loses can defend it; a researcher who
cannot has not examined it. Where a participant category resists
translation — where no available construct carries it without distortion —
that resistance is a finding, and sometimes the paper's actual
contribution is the new construct the resistance forces.

Participants are epistemic agents, not data sources: they generate their own
theoretical interpretations of their conditions. Member checking of the
*translation* (do participants recognize themselves in the second-order
account?) is distinct from member checking of facts, and for claims about
meaning it is often the stronger check.

## Hunting Disconfirmation

For each generalizing claim, actively search the corpus for what troubles
it — negative cases, deviant episodes, the interviews where the pattern
fails. This extends the disconfirming-evidence search that theme
confirmation already performed (in the qualitative-analysis skill), one
level up: the question is no longer "does this theme hold in the data" but
"does the broader claim survive the cases at the theme's edges."

Keep a disconfirmation log:

| Negative case (source ID) | What it shows | Researcher's decision | Consequence for the claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | revise claim / bound scope / category error — researcher chooses | |

Each entry forces exactly one decision, and it belongs to the researcher:
**revise the claim** so it accounts for the case, or **bound the scope** so
the case falls legitimately outside it. Silently dropping the case is the
one unavailable option. A claim record whose disconfirmation log is empty
after a real search is either a remarkably strong claim or an unsearched
corpus — ask which.

Abduction belongs here as well as in target-naming: surprises are engines,
not nuisances (Tavory and Timmermans 2012). The ethnographic version of
strengthening causal explanation — abductive conjecture, then a return to
the data or the field to check it, with explicit strength-of-evidence
assessment — is developed in Snodgrass et al. (2024).

## Naming Rivals

A generalizing claim without a stated rival is a claim nobody tried to
defeat. Require at least one serious rival explanation, stated strongly —
strong enough that a partisan of the rival would sign it — and the evidence
that discriminates.

The discipline here is Yin's (2009) distinction, useful precisely because
it forces explicitness about what kind of reasoning produced the claim:

- **Pattern matching**: the case's observed pattern compared against a
  pattern the theory *predicted* — including the rival's predicted pattern.
- **Explanation building**: propose, compare against evidence, revise,
  compare again — an iterative sequence whose revisions are on the record.
- **Post hoc coherence**: a narrative assembled after the fact that fits
  everything and predicted nothing. This is the one to catch, because it
  reads exactly like the second from the inside.

The probe: "What did you expect to see before you saw it, and what would
the rival have expected?" A claim that cannot answer has not yet been
tested against anything.

Rival table for the claim record:

| Rival explanation | Its strongest evidence in this corpus | What discriminates | Disposition (researcher's) |
|---|---|---|---|

## Disciplining Comparison

Where the warrant leans on similarity — across sites, cases, episodes, or
the study's case and the literature's cases — the similarity itself needs
an explanation before it can count as confirmation. Schnegg (2014), updating
Boas (1896) and the controlled-comparison tradition (Eggan 1954) for a
connected world, distinguishes three reasons two cases look alike:

1. **Diffusion.** The pattern traveled — shared organizers, documents,
   platforms, trainings, media. The cases are one observation.
2. **Common context.** Both cases sit inside the same wider structure — the
   same regulatory regime, market, or colonial history — which produces the
   pattern in both. The similarity is real but tells you about the shared
   context, not about independent recurrence.
3. **Co-variation.** The pattern recurs across cases that differ in the
   relevant conditions and are not connected through the first two routes.
   Only this approximates independent confirmation.

For multi-sited and digital fieldwork this test is not optional: connected
fields are the default, and apparent replications usually reflect routes 1
or 2. The repair is not to abandon the comparison but to say what it
actually shows — and to look for co-variation across whatever *does* differ
between the connected cases.

Triangulation and member checking do different work and are not
interchangeable (Lincoln and Guba 1985): triangulation tests the accuracy
of specific data items across sources, methods, or investigators; member
checking evaluates the credibility of reconstructions and interpretations
with the people they are about. A warrant can need either, both, or —
rarely — neither; problem-driven, not accumulative.

## The Chain of Evidence

The claim record's authority rests on an auditable chain. Every generalizing
claim should be traceable through:

```
Claim (kind declared)
  ↓
Analytic memo naming the mechanism, category, or working hypothesis
  ↓
Linked evidence set
  • confirmed themes (with their codebook version)
  • fieldnote and interview segments (source IDs)
  • observed events, documents, artifacts
  • quantitative or biomedical complements, if used — as lenses, not validators
  ↓
Checks
  • disconfirmation log (with dispositions)
  • rival table (with dispositions)
  • comparison discipline (diffusion / common context / co-variation)
  • triangulation and member checks, where warranted
  ↓
Scope conditions
  ↓
Uncertainty statement
```

The audit-trail principle (Lincoln and Guba 1985) applies with force at
this altitude: a reader should be able to reconstruct how a datum became an
interpretation, how the interpretation became a claim, and where
uncertainty entered. Reflexive notes belong in the record, not in private
residue — the researcher's position shapes what was visible, and reporting
it is part of inferential validity, not a stylistic appendix (Abu-Lughod
1991).
