# Ethnographic Sources

Chicago has templates for published works. Much of what an ethnography cites
was never published: the author's own field notes, conversations with
people who agreed to be studied, community archives, recordings, and posts.
For these, citation form and confidentiality are the same decision. The
convention below is the default; the consent record overrides it.

## The rule that governs everything here

**How a participant may be named is settled by consent, not by citation
style.** Before writing a citation that names, quotes, or points to a
person you met as a researcher, establish what their consent covers: real
name, pseudonym, or no attribution at all; quotation or paraphrase only;
whether the source may be located. If that is unclear, route to the
`informed-consent` skill. Do not resolve it by choosing whatever citation
form looks most scholarly.

## Your own field notes

Cite in the text or a note, with no list entry: (field notes, March 12,
2019). The reader cannot consult them, so a list entry points at nothing.
Describe the corpus (dates, sites, how notes were written) once, in the
methods section.

## Interviews you conducted

- **In the text or a note, not in the reference list.** A reference-list
  entry turns a participant, under a pseudonym or not, into an author and
  creates a findable record.
- **Pseudonym form.** Give the pseudonym and the date, and say once in the
  methods section that names are pseudonyms: (Interview with "Dolores,"
  June 4, 2021) or "Dolores told me in June 2021…". Some venues want the
  pseudonym marked on first use; follow the venue.
- **Dates and places can identify.** A date plus a small fieldsite plus a
  role can point to one person. Give the precision the argument needs, not
  the precision the notebook holds. The `fieldwork-methods` skill's
  deductive-disclosure guidance applies here.
- **Named participants.** When someone has chosen to be named (an expert, a
  public figure, a collaborator who wants credit), cite them by name as a
  personal communication, and make sure the consent record says so.

## Archived interviews and oral histories

An interview deposited in an archive is a published-enough source: cite it
like an archival item, with the narrator, the interviewer, the date, the
collection, and the repository. Follow the repository's preferred citation
and any access restrictions it records. Some archives restrict quotation or
require permission for named citation even when the recording is open.

## Archives

Cite the collection in the reference list or bibliography and the item in
the text or note (see `notes-bibliography.md` for the note form). Colonial
and missionary archives often describe people and communities in terms
they did not choose; quote the catalog description where it matters to the
argument, and do not adopt it as your own description.

## Community knowledge and knowledge holders

Chicago has no category for knowledge held and shared by an Elder,
Knowledge Keeper, or community authority. Practice varies by venue and by
community, and some communities have protocols about whether and how
knowledge is attributed.

- Ask how the person or community wants to be cited, and record the answer
  with the consent material.
- Where named attribution is agreed, cite as a personal communication with
  the name, the community or nation as they state it, and the date, and add
  the role or title they use.
- Some venues ask for a reference-list entry for knowledge holders. Follow
  the venue only where the community has agreed to that form.
- Do not cite restricted or sacred knowledge at all without explicit
  permission, whatever the citation form.

## Participants' social media posts and online material

A quoted post is a searchable string: pasting it into a search engine can
find its author, and a citation with a handle or URL makes it certain.

- Treat public posts by private individuals as participant material, not as
  published sources, unless the author is a public figure posting publicly
  in that role.
- Where consent covers quotation with attribution, cite the handle, the
  date, and the platform as the venue prefers.
- Where it does not, paraphrase, or quote and alter the wording with a
  statement in the methods section that quotations from online material
  have been modified to prevent identification. Do not give a URL.
- The `digital-computational-methods` skill covers the ethics of online
  fieldwork more fully.

## Non-English sources

- **Titles in other languages** take sentence-style capitalization (only the
  first word and proper nouns capitalized), whatever the English convention.
  Keep diacritics.
- **A translated title** may follow in square brackets, roman type and not
  italic: *Essai sur le don* [The gift].
- **Your own translations** of quoted material are noted once, usually in
  the first note or the methods section: "Translations are mine unless
  otherwise noted."
- **Transliteration.** Use one system consistently (for example ALA-LC for
  Russian or Arabic), say which, and keep the author's spellings of names as
  they appear in their published work.

## Reprinted and translated classics

The original date matters in anthropology, because readers place an
argument in its moment. Show it: (Mauss [1925] 1990) in author-date, or a
closing "Originally published in 1925" in notes and bibliography. Cite the
pages of the edition actually used.

## Data sets and archived research materials

Cite a deposited data set (for example in Qualitative Data Repository or
another archive) with its creator, year, title, repository, and DOI, in the
reference list. If the deposit is restricted, say so; the citation still
stands.
