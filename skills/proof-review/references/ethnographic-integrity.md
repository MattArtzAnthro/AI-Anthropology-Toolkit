# Ethnographic Integrity at Proof Stage

The pass that a general proofreading procedure does not contain.

Production treats a manuscript as text to be regularized. Ethnographic
writing contains material that must not be regularized: names that were
chosen to conceal, speech that was transcribed rather than composed,
orthographies that carry a language's own conventions, and typographic
choices that were arguments. Every one of these survives copyediting and
typesetting only by accident, and every one of them is invisible to a
reader who is checking for typos.

Each item below is checked by name. None of it is caught by reading.

## Pseudonyms, Place Names, and Anonymization

**The copyeditor's consistency sweep is the most dangerous thing that
happens to a pseudonymized manuscript.** Consistency tools cannot
distinguish a variant that is an inconsistency from a variant that is a
deliberate anonymization decision.

Check:

- **Every pseudonym, at every occurrence, against the manuscript.** A
  regularization that collapses "Doña Marta" and "Marta" into one form may
  be erasing a distinction the author made about who addresses her how.
- **Whether any real name appears where the manuscript had a pseudonym.**
  This happens through a copyeditor's fact-checking pass, through a
  find-and-replace applied in the wrong direction, and through a comment
  or query in which the real name was used.
- **Place names against the manuscript, not against a gazetteer.** A
  fieldsite spelled the way the manuscript spells it may be a deliberate
  approximation. A house-style correction to the official spelling, or the
  addition of a region or country the manuscript withheld, can re-identify
  a site that was anonymized by imprecision.
- **Institutional names, job titles, and specific numbers.** A clinic
  described by type in the manuscript and by name in the proof, a role
  made more specific, or an exact staff count restored where the
  manuscript gave a range: each narrows the population that could be
  described.
- **Author queries and editorial notes for identifying detail.** A query
  reading "AU: is this the same clinic as on p. 4?" carries the
  identification into the file, and unresolved queries sometimes print.
- **Acknowledgments, against who consented to be named.** Community
  members, research assistants, and interlocutors are named in
  acknowledgments under different agreements than colleagues are, and a
  press's standard acknowledgments formatting does not know that.
- **Maps, photographs, and site figures** for detail the consent agreement
  required removed, and for a redaction or blur that did not survive
  re-rendering at print resolution.

**Escalation rule.** Any finding in this section is escalated
individually, never batched, and never resolved by inference from the two
files. The consent agreement is not in either document. Name the
consequence, route the consent question to the informed-consent skill, and
stop. Classify as critical until the author says otherwise.

## Quoted Speech and Transcription

**A compositor's or copyeditor's cleanup is the most dangerous thing that
happens to a transcribed quotation.** Participant speech is evidence. The
standard for it is not whether it reads well.

Check every block quotation and every quoted phrase for:

- **Regularized grammar.** Non-standard agreement, tense, or word order
  in a participant's speech that has been made standard. This is the most
  common and least visible: it looks like good copyediting and it changes
  the record. It also changes how a speaker reads, which is an
  ethnographic and a political problem, not a stylistic one.
- **Repaired disfluency.** Repetitions, false starts, and filler removed.
  Where the author kept them, they were kept for a reason.
- **Ellipses.** Inside a quotation an ellipsis marks an omission. A
  compositor's conversion of spacing or character is normally mechanical,
  but check that no ellipsis was added, removed, or moved, and that the
  author's declared convention for elision within versus between sentences
  survived.
- **Square brackets.** Bracketed insertions mark the author's additions to
  a speaker's words. Brackets silently dropped make an insertion read as
  testimony. Brackets converted to parentheses do the same.
- **Transcription conventions.** Overlapping speech, timed pauses, sotto
  voce marking, latching, and any notation system declared in the methods
  section. Compositors regularize notation they do not recognize, and a
  conversation-analytic transcript can be flattened into prose without
  anyone noticing that a claim in the analysis no longer has evidence
  under it.
- **Attribution lines and speaker labels**, including whether a speaker
  label was changed to a pseudonym's variant form.
- **Translation apparatus.** Where the manuscript gives the original and
  the translation, check that both survived, in the same relationship,
  with the same emphasis, and that a space-saving cut did not remove the
  original.

**Any change inside quotation marks is critical or substantive until
established otherwise.** A change to a participant's words is not an
editorial matter.

## Diacritics, Orthography, and Non-Latin Script

The proof is where an orthography meets a font that may not contain it.

Check:

- **Diacritics on participant names, place names, and vernacular terms**,
  character by character rather than by appearance. Acute, grave,
  circumflex, caron, breve, macron, ogonek, and cedilla are distinguished
  at the codepoint, and a rendered page at screen resolution is not
  sufficient to tell some of them apart.
- **Glottal stops and ejectives.** The modifier letter apostrophe and the
  saltillo are routinely converted to a straight or curly typographic
  apostrophe by a smart-quotes pass. This is a spelling error in the
  language concerned, and it is the single most common orthographic
  failure in proofs of work with Indigenous-language material.
- **Tone marks, length marks, and combining characters**, which can be
  dropped, doubled, or displaced during font fallback.
- **IPA and phonetic notation**, which frequently falls back to a
  substitute font mid-string.
- **Non-Latin script**, for correct rendering, correct directionality, and
  correct joining behavior, checked against the manuscript by someone who
  reads it. Where nobody in the session reads it, that goes in the **Not
  examined** register rather than being passed as fine.
- **Whether a character was dropped entirely.** A missing glyph often
  renders as nothing at all rather than as a visible box, which means the
  word simply looks slightly short.

Report these under the font-substitution exception in
[comparison-method.md](comparison-method.md): a typeface change is
suppressed, a glyph identity change is reported.

## Italicization of Vernacular Terms

Many presses italicize non-English terms on first use, or throughout.
Many authors deliberately do not, on the argument that italicizing a
language marks it as foreign to the reader and reproduces the othering the
work is arguing against.

That is a position, and a house-style pass reverses it silently.

Check whether italicization of vernacular terms in the proof matches the
manuscript. Where it does not, classify as requiring the author's
confirmation rather than as an acceptable house-style change, and say what
the change implies, because the author may have made the choice
deliberately and may not have told the press.

The same applies in reverse: where the manuscript italicized a term on
first use only and the proof italicizes throughout, or the reverse.

## Figures, Captions, and Callouts

Beyond the general production checks:

- **Kinship diagrams, genealogies, and network figures** for symbol
  integrity. Kinship notation uses shapes and lines carrying specific
  meaning, and re-rendering, rescaling, or a substituted symbol font can
  change what a diagram asserts about a relationship.
- **Figure renumbering against the final chapter number**, and every
  in-text callout with it. Renumbering that leaves the callouts behind is
  the ordinary case, not the exception.
- **Caption-to-figure attachment**, verified by reading the caption against
  the image rather than by checking that the numbers are sequential.
- **Field photographs** for resolution, cropping that removed a consented
  boundary, and any face or identifying feature that a redaction was
  supposed to cover.
- **Tables of demographic or field data** for column alignment lost in
  typesetting, which silently reassigns values to the wrong variable.
- **Alt text**, per the alt-text rule in
  [comparison-method.md](comparison-method.md), with the additional check
  that alt text does not describe detail the figure itself was redacted to
  remove.

## What This Pass Does Not Decide

It reports. It does not decide whether a restored detail breaches an
anonymization commitment, whether a regularized quotation still represents
what a participant said, or whether an italicization change is acceptable
to the author.

Those are the author's, and the reasons are in the SKILL.md division of
labour.
