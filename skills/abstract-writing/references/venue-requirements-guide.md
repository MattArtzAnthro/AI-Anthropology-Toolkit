# Venue Requirements, Titles, and Keywords

General rules for meeting a venue's abstract requirements, and for building
the title and keyword set that travel with it. For the abstract's internal
architecture, see
[abstract-construction-guide.md](abstract-construction-guide.md).

**This guide deliberately carries no table of named journals and their word
limits.** Limits, keyword counts, and structured-abstract rules change between
submission cycles, and a stale number in a reference file is worse than no
number, because it reads as authoritative. Verify the requirement against the
journal's current author guidelines every time. What follows is the shape of
the requirement and how to find it.

## Finding the Real Requirement

**Where it lives, in order of reliability.**

1. The journal's own author guidelines or submission guidelines page,
   published by the journal or its society.
2. The submission portal itself. Portals enforce a character or word count at
   upload, and that enforced number governs regardless of what the guidelines
   page says. Where the two disagree, the portal wins, and the discrepancy is
   worth an email to the editorial office.
3. A recent issue. Ten abstracts from the last two issues give the operative
   length, register, and structure faster than any guidance document, and they
   show what the editors actually accept.

**What to extract.** Word or character limit; whether the count includes the
title; keyword count and whether a controlled vocabulary is required;
paragraph or structured format; whether a separate lay summary, significance
statement, or graphical abstract is required; and whether the abstract is
submitted in a portal field, in the manuscript file, or both.

**When the guidelines cannot be reached.** Publisher sites frequently block
automated access. Say so plainly rather than substituting a remembered number:
report which pages were tried and what blocked them, fall back on the
last-two-issues method for an operative estimate, label it as an estimate, and
tell the author to confirm before submission.

## Typical Ranges

These are planning estimates for drafting before the requirement is confirmed,
not requirements themselves.

| Venue type | Typical abstract | Typical keywords |
|---|---|---|
| Sociocultural and linguistic anthropology journals | 100-200 words, single paragraph | 5-9 |
| Generalist and flagship disciplinary journals | 150-250 words, single paragraph | 0-8 |
| Medical anthropology and health-adjacent journals | 150-300 words, sometimes structured | 3-6, sometimes controlled vocabulary |
| Archaeology and biological anthropology journals | 150-300 words, results-forward | 4-8 |
| Interdisciplinary and area-studies journals | 150-250 words | 4-8 |
| Edited volume chapters | 100-200 words, or none; the editor sets it | Usually none |
| Thesis and dissertation abstracts | Set by the university, often 350 words or a page | Sometimes required by the repository |

Two patterns hold across almost all of them: the tightest limits sit in
sociocultural journals, and the longest sit where the abstract has to serve a
biomedical or policy readership that will never open the article.

## Structured Abstracts

Most ethnographic journals use a single unstructured paragraph. Structured
abstracts, with headings such as Background, Methods, Results, and
Conclusions, appear where a journal serves clinical, public health, or policy
audiences, and they are increasingly common in medical anthropology venues
that index in biomedical databases.

The five moves map onto the standard headings, but not cleanly, and the seams
are where interpretive work gets damaged:

| Move | Structured heading | What goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Scene | Background | Compresses to a setting description and loses its analytical edge |
| Problem | Background | Reads as a literature gap rather than a puzzle |
| Evidence | Methods | Fits well; state design, extent, and site |
| Claim | Results | The hardest seam: an interpretive claim is not a result |
| Stake | Conclusions | Fits well |

**Handling the Results seam.** A reading is not a result, and forcing it into a
Results heading produces either a flattened finding ("participants reported
frustration with waiting") or an unsupported assertion. Two workable moves:
put the ethnographic pattern under Results and the interpretive claim under
Conclusions, or state the claim under Results in evidentiary form ("Waiting
functioned across all three clinics as a rationing mechanism rather than an
administrative byproduct"). Which to choose depends on how the journal's own
recent abstracts handle interpretation; check them.

If a journal requires headings the article's argument genuinely cannot fill,
that is worth raising with the editor before submission rather than solving
silently in the abstract.

## Keywords

Keywords are the article's index entry. They are read by databases and search
engines, not by people, and they are the cheapest discoverability the author
will ever get.

**Rules.**

- Supply exactly the number requested. Fewer looks careless; more gets
  truncated at an arbitrary point that the author did not choose.
- Draw them from the analytic vocabulary the title omits. A keyword set that
  restates the title indexes the article once rather than three times.
- Cover four axes: the topic, the theoretical frame, the method or evidence
  type, and the region or population. An article on clinic waiting in Lagos
  might carry: waiting; obstetric care; temporality; ethnography; Nigeria.
- Prefer established terms over coinages for most of the set. A coined term
  earns one slot, because nobody is searching for it yet, and the rest of the
  set is what brings readers who have never heard of it.
- Avoid single broad nouns. "Culture," "society," and "identity" match
  everything and therefore retrieve nothing.
- Use multi-word phrases where the field uses them: "obstetric racism," not
  "racism" plus "obstetrics."
- Check whether the journal requires a controlled vocabulary. Biomedically
  indexed venues sometimes require MeSH terms, in which case free choice is
  not on offer and the closest available terms have to be selected
  deliberately.

## Titles

The title does more indexing work than the keywords and is the only element
every reader sees.

**The two-part pattern** dominates anthropology and works: an evocative or
quoted phrase, then a colon, then a descriptive subtitle carrying the search
terms.

> "We Just Wait": Obstetric Delay and the Distribution of Harm in Two Lagos
> Clinics

The first half earns attention; the second half tells a database what the
article is about. A title with only the first half is unfindable; a title with
only the second half is forgettable.

**Rules.**

- The subtitle carries the terms a reader would actually search: the topic,
  the site or population, and the analytic frame.
- Make the title intelligible outside the subfield. A reader who does not know
  the theoretical tradition should still know what the article studies.
- Put a coined term in the title only if the article's contribution is the
  term. It buys recognition later and costs discoverability now.
- Keep the quoted fragment short and let it do real work; a participant's
  phrase that names the article's problem is worth the space, and one chosen
  for color is not.
- Check the title against the anonymization commitments. Naming a site in the
  title is a permanent disclosure.
- Do not pose the article as a question unless it answers it in the abstract.

## Other Required Elements

Some venues require more than the abstract, and these are frequently
overlooked until the portal refuses the submission.

- **Lay or plain-language summary.** A separate short text for non-specialist
  readers. Not the abstract with the terminology removed; it is written for
  someone who does not know why the question matters, so it needs its own
  stake sentence. Route substantial public-facing rewriting to the
  public-engagement skill.
- **Significance or impact statement.** Two or three sentences on what the
  work changes. It restates the stake move for an audience deciding whether
  to fund, cover, or teach the work.
- **Graphical abstract.** A single figure standing in for the argument. Common
  in interdisciplinary and biomedical venues, rare in ethnographic ones.
- **Author contribution and data availability statements.** Not abstract work,
  but frequently required in the same portal step; the research-writing skill
  covers ethics and data statements.

## Reuse Across Contexts

The same article generates several abstracts, and they are not
interchangeable. Rewriting rather than truncating is the rule: an abstract cut
to fit a different venue usually loses the move that venue most needed.

- **Journal to conference.** Conference abstracts promise a talk, not an
  article, and they are often written before the analysis is finished. The
  conference-materials skill owns this genre, including panel and roundtable
  proposals.
- **Journal to grant.** A funder's summary answers to review criteria and
  usually needs a broader-impacts sentence the journal abstract has no reason
  to carry. The grant-proposal skill owns funder-facing summaries.
- **Journal to repository or profile page.** Institutional repositories,
  ORCID, and personal sites usually accept the journal abstract unchanged;
  check whether the publisher's copyright transfer covers posting it, which is
  common but not universal.
- **Journal to public writing.** A different genre, not a shorter abstract.
  Use the public-engagement skill.
