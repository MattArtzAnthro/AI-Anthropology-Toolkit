# Abstract, Title, and Keywords for Anthropology Submissions

Write the abstract, title, and keywords that carry an anthropological
manuscript into review and, afterward, into the search results where most of
its readers will meet it. These three elements are frequently the only parts
of the article that circulate freely, and at many journals they are the only
parts a prospective reviewer sees before deciding whether to accept the
invitation. American Ethnologist states this outright in its submission
guidelines: invitations to reviewers include only the title, abstract, and
keywords, so those three have to do the persuading alone.

An abstract is not a summary of the article. It is a compressed argument with
an ethnographic scene in it. The generic social science formula — background,
methods, results, conclusions — will produce a competent abstract for a paper
that reports results, and a flat one for a paper whose contribution is a
reading. Anthropological abstracts carry three things the formula has no slot
for: a situation named concretely enough that a reader can see it, an
evidence credential that says what kind of fieldwork stands behind the claim,
and often a coined term whose only public definition is the one sentence the
abstract gives it.

The abstract is also the first place an anonymization commitment breaks. A
manuscript that pseudonymizes a clinic, a village, or a firm, and an abstract
that names the region, the institution type, and the years precisely enough
to identify it, have between them undone the protection the manuscript
promised — and the abstract is the part that gets indexed permanently.

## What This Skill Will and Will Not Do

This skill adopts the Friction by Design conventions at Tier 2; the canonical
form is defined in [skills/DESIGN.md](../DESIGN.md).

**Will not do, under any setting.**

- Decide what the manuscript's contribution is. If the claim sentence does
  not already exist somewhere in the manuscript, the abstract is not the
  place to invent it; use the paper-planning skill.
- State a finding, a scope, or a generalization the manuscript does not
  support. Compression is where a scoped claim quietly becomes a general
  one, and the abstract is the version that travels.
- Coin the article's term, or write its definition. Naming a concept is the
  contribution itself.
- Decide how much of the fieldsite may be identified. What can be named
  without breaking a consent or anonymization commitment is the author's
  judgment, made against what participants were told.
- Choose which of several true claims the article should lead with. The
  skill lays out the candidates and asks which one the article actually
  delivers.

An abstract whose claim came from the machine is one the author cannot
defend under review, because the reviewer's first question is why this claim
and not the neighboring one.

**Will do, on request.**

- Cut a draft to a word limit, and report what was dropped rather than
  silently dropping it.
- Restate the author's own claim sentence in abstract register.
- Generate candidate titles and keyword sets from terms already present in
  the manuscript.
- Run the promise check and the standing checks below, and report what
  fired.
- Reformat a paragraph abstract into a venue's structured template, or back.

Anything produced this way is marked in the output under the record register
described below.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Establish the venue and the manuscript

Two facts govern everything downstream: the word limit with its keyword rule,
and whether the manuscript is finished. Collect both together; they are facts,
not decisions.

If the venue is known, get its current requirement rather than working from
memory or from a table. Load the venue requirements guide, which carries the
verification procedure and what to do when the guidelines are behind a
paywall or a block.

If the manuscript is not yet drafted, say so and stop. An abstract written
before the article exists is a plan, and the article will not match it. Route
to paper-planning to settle the claim, or to research-writing to draft.

### Step 2: Locate the claim in the manuscript

Find the sentence in the manuscript where the author states what the article
argues. It is usually in the introduction, sometimes only in the conclusion,
and occasionally in neither, which is itself the finding to report.

Quote the sentence back and ask one confirm-or-revise question: is this what
the article argues? Do not paraphrase it into something stronger. If the
author cannot point to such a sentence, route to paper-planning rather than
drafting around the gap.

### Step 3: Draft the five moves

Load the abstract construction guide. Draft each move as a distinct sentence
or clause before worrying about flow:

1. **Scene** — the situation, named as concretely as the anonymization
   commitments allow.
2. **Problem** — what is analytically at issue there.
3. **Evidence** — the fieldwork, archive, or corpus, with its kind and
   extent.
4. **Claim** — what the article argues, including the definition of any
   coined term.
5. **Stake** — which conversation the article enters and what changes in it.

Short limits merge moves rather than dropping them. A 150-word abstract that
has dropped the evidence move reads, to an anthropologist, as a paper with no
fieldwork behind it.

### Step 4: Run the promise check

Every claim in the abstract must map to a specific section of the manuscript
that delivers it. Walk the abstract sentence by sentence and name the section
each one draws on. Report any sentence that maps to nothing: either the
manuscript is missing the work the abstract promises, or the abstract is
promising work the author did not do. Which of those it is, is the author's
call.

### Step 5: Cut to the limit

Compress against the guide's priority order. Report the cuts as a list rather
than presenting only the shortened text, because the first thing lost under a
tight limit is usually the scope condition that kept the claim honest.

### Step 6: Title and keywords

Build the title from the terms the abstract already uses, and the keywords
from terms the title does not. A keyword set that restates the title indexes
the article once instead of three times. The venue requirements guide carries
the construction patterns and the count rules.

### Step 7: Report the standing checks

The abstract is a durable artifact, so these run without being asked, and the
output says in one sentence what ran and what fired:

- Word count against the venue limit; keyword count against the venue rule.
- No citations, and no acronym used before it is expanded.
- Every coined term that appears is defined in the abstract itself.
- Every pseudonym matches the manuscript's, and no place, institution, or
  date pair identifies a site the manuscript pseudonymizes.
- Every abstract sentence maps to a manuscript section (the promise check).
- The scope of the claim in the abstract matches the scope in the
  manuscript's conclusion.

A check that could not run is reported as unrun, not as passed. A fired check
names a commitment the abstract implies; whether that commitment is the
author's is the author's to say.

## Parameters

Established once and carried, not re-asked; see the shared framework in
[skills/DESIGN.md](../DESIGN.md).

- **Genre** — research article, thesis or dissertation abstract, book
  chapter, review essay. Thesis abstracts run longer and answer to a
  university's format rules rather than a journal's.
- **Epistemic stance** — determines what counts as the evidence move and how
  contribution is framed. An interpretive abstract claims a reading; a
  political economy abstract claims a relation; a computational abstract
  claims a pattern and owes its corpus size.
- **Subfield** — archaeology and biological anthropology abstracts report
  results and often permit numbers; sociocultural and linguistic abstracts
  more often claim a reading.
- **Risk posture** — high-surveillance or politically sensitive work
  constrains how specifically the scene move can be written.
- **Formality register** — a working abstract for a co-author reads
  differently from the one that goes into the submission portal.

Say which parameter drove an output when it drove one.

## Guardrails

- **The abstract may not exceed the manuscript.** No claim, no scope, no
  method that is not in the manuscript.
- **Anonymization is checked in the abstract, not only in the article.**
  Cross-check against the manuscript's own commitments; the
  research-writing skill carries the anonymization guide.
- **No citations.** An abstract stands alone; it names positions rather than
  citing them.
- **Generalization claims stay scoped.** If the manuscript's scope
  conditions were settled with the ethnographic-generalization skill, the
  abstract inherits them verbatim rather than rounding them up.
- **Verify the venue's current requirement.** Limits and keyword rules
  change between submission cycles, and a table in a reference file is a
  planning estimate, never the requirement.

## Record Register

Output carries two registers, and neither is tidied:

- **Unresolved.** What the author still has to settle: which claim leads,
  how much of the site may be named, whether the manuscript delivers a
  promise the abstract makes.
- **Assembled rather than authored.** Which sentences were the machine's
  assembly of the author's own material — cuts, reformatting, candidate
  titles and keywords — as distinct from what the author decided.

An abstract that comes back with nothing unresolved after a full pass has
usually been tidied rather than finished.

## Common Failure Modes

| Failure | What it looks like | Repair |
|---------|-------------------|--------|
| The aspirational abstract | Promises the article the author wishes they had written | Run the promise check; cut to what the manuscript delivers |
| Background creep | Three sentences of literature before the article appears | One sentence of scene; the conversation goes in the stake move |
| The undefined coinage | A new term named but never defined | Define it in the sentence that introduces it, or cut it |
| The de-anonymizing abstract | Region, institution type, and years together identify a pseudonymized site | Widen the scene move until the site is not recoverable |
| Scope inflation | "Migrants" where the article studied one town's returnees | Restore the manuscript's own scope conditions |
| The evidence-free claim | No indication of what fieldwork stands behind the reading | Add the evidence move; it is the discipline's credential |
| Keyword redundancy | Keywords restate the title word for word | Draw keywords from the analytic vocabulary the title omits |
| Theory name-dropping | Names a theorist the article barely uses | Cut; reviewers check |
| The register as disclaimer | Everything marked "assembled," so nothing is owned | The register marks machine assembly, not authorship generally |

## Examples

**A dissertation chapter going out as an article.** The author has a finished
draft and a 150-word limit. The claim sentence exists in the conclusion but
not the introduction. Step 2 surfaces that, the author confirms it, and the
abstract is built on it — which also tells the author their introduction is
promising something else.

**A tight limit and a coined term.** At 150 words the author wants both the
term and its definition. The evidence move compresses to a clause ("across
fourteen months in two clinics"), the stake move merges into the claim, and
the cut list shows the author exactly which scope condition went, so they can
decide whether to spend words restoring it.

**A structured-abstract venue.** A medical anthropology journal requires
Background, Methods, Results, and Conclusions headings. The interpretive
claim does not fit "Results." The guide's mapping table shows which move goes
under which heading and where the argument has to be restated, rather than
forcing the paragraph version into slots.

**Nothing to abstract yet.** The author asks for an abstract for a paper they
have outlined but not written. The skill declines, says why, and routes to
paper-planning.
