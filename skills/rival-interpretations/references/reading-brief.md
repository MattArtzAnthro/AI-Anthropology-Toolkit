# The Reading Brief

What each isolated reader receives, and the two rules that decide whether its
output is admitted.

## Isolation

One subagent per position. Each receives the material, its assigned position,
and the claim under test. None sees the others' readings, and none is told
which position the researcher holds.

Withholding the home position matters. A reader told which position the
researcher favors will either flatter it or overcorrect against it, and
neither is the reading the material would have produced.

## The brief

```
You are reading this material from one analytical position and arguing what
that position sees.

Position: [name from the registry]
What this position attends to: [description and the attending half of the
  registry's modifier, with its coding instructions removed]
Claim under test: [the claim, in the researcher's own words]
Material: [path or text]

Produce four things.

1. YOUR READING. What this position sees in this material that bears on the
   claim. Argue it. Do not survey what the position is about.

2. WHAT IT COSTS THE CLAIM. Whether your reading weakens the claim, refines
   it, supports it for different reasons than the researcher gave, or leaves
   it untouched. All four are real answers. "Leaves it untouched" is the
   answer when it is true.

3. YOUR FALSIFIER. What would have to be true in this material for YOUR
   reading to fail. Not a theoretical caveat: something checkable in the
   text, the data, or the instrument.

4. YOUR SPANS. The passages your reading rests on, quoted.

If you cannot produce item 3 from this material, say so and stop. Do not
substitute a theoretical objection. A position with nothing at stake here has
a real answer, and it is silence.
```

## The falsifier rule

**No reading is admitted without a falsifier drawn from the material.**

The falsifier is the load-bearing part of the whole instrument. It does three
jobs at once.

**It separates argument from vocabulary.** A reading that cannot say what
would defeat it is usually a restatement of the claim in the position's
dialect. That reads as disagreement and is not.

**It polices the roster after the fact.** A position with nothing genuinely
at stake cannot produce a falsifier from this material. Its silence is the
correct outcome, and the record names which position produced nothing and
why. This is why no authored debate posture per position is needed: the rule
catches thin seats better than authored content would.

**It is often the most useful output.** A falsifier is a thing the researcher
can go and check. In practice these are the lines a researcher acts on first:
hand the instrument to someone who did not build it, substitute the vendor
and see whether the capacity survives, re-read the three chunks the coding
run disagreed about.

A falsifier is admissible when someone could, in principle, do it. It does
not have to be cheap.

Not admissible: "this reading fails if the tradition it draws on is wrong,"
"this fails if the researcher intended something else," "this fails if the
material is unrepresentative." The first is not about the material, the
second cannot be checked against anything, and the third is true of all
ethnography and therefore discriminates nothing.

## The span rule

**No rebuttal without a span.** A rebuttal quotes the claim it attacks and
points at the passage that defeats it.

Rebuttals fire only where two readings make incompatible claims about the
same span. Two readings that are simply different are not in conflict, and
firing a rebuttal between them produces one sentence of polite agreement at
the cost of a full dispatch.

Incompatible means both cannot be true of that passage. A reading that says a
passage deletes the labor behind an instrument and a reading that says the
same passage locates the instrument in a relationship are incompatible: they
disagree about what the passage is doing. A reading about ownership and a
reading about phenomenal experience, pointed at different passages, are
merely different.

## The rebuttal brief

```
Another position has read the same material. Its reading, its falsifier, and
its spans are below.

Where does it make a claim about a span you also read, that cannot be true
alongside yours?

For each such point: quote its claim, quote the span, and say what defeats
it. Concede where it is right, and say exactly what you are conceding —
wording, or substance. Those are different concessions and the record keeps
them apart.

If there is no incompatible claim, say so in one line. Manufactured
disagreement is worse than none, because it survives into the record looking
like a finding.
```

## Concessions

A conceded point is recorded with its kind, because the two kinds have
different consequences.

**A wording concession** means the reading was right and the sentence
carrying it was careless. It generates a defect.

**A substance concession** means the position gave ground on what it holds.
It usually resolves what would otherwise have stayed open, and it is the
strongest result this instrument produces.

A reader that concedes wording while rejecting substance has done its job
precisely. Record both halves. Collapsing them into "conceded" loses the
thing the researcher most needs to know.
