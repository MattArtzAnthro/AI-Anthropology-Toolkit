# Sorting Comments by What They Demand

Comments are not equal, and treating them as a single queue is what makes a
marked-up manuscript feel like an ordeal. Forty of them are corrections.
Four are arguments. One asks for something the author cannot give. The sort
is what separates these, and it is the first gate.

Every comment goes into exactly one kind. When a comment could be two,
place it in the more demanding one and say why.

## The five kinds

### Mechanical

A typo, a citation format, house style, a missing page number, a date, an
inconsistent spelling. Nothing turns on the author's judgment.

Recognition cues: the comment names a rule rather than a reading. Short.
Often clustered in the references and the notes.

Handling: list them, confirm the batch, move on. These never consume an
exchange. A mechanical comment the author disputes is usually not mechanical,
so let a dispute promote it.

### Local judgment

Word choice, register, a hedge to remove, a sentence to cut, a paragraph to
tighten. Real judgment, small stakes, and reversible.

Recognition cues: "too long," "wordy," "clunky," "do you need this?", a
struck sentence with no explanation.

Handling: group by section and confirm as a group, with individual dialogue
available on request. Watch for the pattern where thirty local comments in
one section are one structural comment the editor did not write down.

### Structural

This section does not belong here. Reorder these. This subsection is doing
two jobs. Move the theory forward. Cut the second case.

Recognition cues: the comment refers to the document rather than the
sentence. Often placed at a heading. Often repeated in different words in
several places.

Handling: individually, against the anchored span. Collapse recurrences
first, decide once, then apply. See below.

### Argumentative

The claim does not hold. The evidence does not reach that conclusion. You
need to engage this literature. This reads as description rather than
analysis. Who else says this?

Recognition cues: the comment disputes something rather than requesting
something. Frequently phrased as a question.

Handling: individually, against the anchored span, and never resolved by
softening the claim unless the author decides the claim was wrong.
Hedging a contested claim into vagueness satisfies the comment and damages
the paper.

### Ethically constrained

The comment cannot be accepted as written without breaching a consent
agreement, deanonymizing a participant or a fieldsite, or inventing
ethnographic detail. This is the kind that makes this an anthropological
problem rather than a document-handling one.

Recognition cues, which look entirely ordinary:

- "Which village is this?" "Name the organization." "Which department?"
- "Who said this?" "Is this the same person as on page 4?"
- "This scene needs more detail, make it vivid."
- "Can you say more about what happened next?"
- "Add the date."
- "A photograph here would help."
- "Say how many people were in the room."

Each is a reasonable editorial request. Each may also be impossible.
Composite characters, altered place names, changed occupations, and shifted
dates are ordinary protective devices, and an editor cannot see them. The
request to make a scene vivid is the most dangerous, because the fluent
response is to supply detail that was never in the field notes.

Handling, and it does not vary:

1. Name the constraint. Consent scope, anonymization commitment, IRB
   protocol, or the absence of the material in the record.
2. Establish what is actually true, with the author. The skill does not know
   what the consent covered.
3. Decide what can be offered instead. A category rather than a name. A
   region rather than a town. A characterization of the scene rather than
   its particulars. A footnote explaining that identifying detail has been
   altered.
4. Write the response to the editor. This kind always produces language for
   the letter, whatever the outcome, because an unexplained refusal reads as
   evasion and an explained one usually ends the matter.

Where the question is what the consent actually permits, that is the
informed-consent skill's work. Where the question is which details are
identifying, that is the research-writing skill's anonymization guide.

## The sort table

Propose the whole sort as one table with one confirm-or-revise question.
Not one question per comment, which is the interrogation that exhausts, and
not a silent classification, which is the machine deciding.

```markdown
| # | Section | Anchor (short) | Comment | Kind | Proposed |
|---|---------|----------------|---------|------|----------|
| 12 | Methods | "fourteen months in the valley" | Which valley? | Ethically constrained | Offer region, not town |
| 13 | Methods | "participants reported" | How many? | Mechanical | Accept |
| 27 | Analysis | "this suggests a shift" | Evidence does not carry this | Argumentative | Discuss |
```

Then one question: "Does this sort look right, and is there anything in the
wrong row?"

## Collapsing cross-cutting comments

The same objection often appears in six places, phrased differently each
time. Deciding it six times produces six inconsistent decisions.

Collapse before the dialogue, not after. Group by what is being objected to
rather than by where the comment sits. Present the group as one item with
all six anchors listed, decide once, and record the decision against every
comment id in the group so none of them goes missing from the letter.

A cluster of six is also a signal. Where the recurring objection is about
the argument rather than the prose, the paper-planning skill is the right
place to take it, and patching six sections will not settle it.

## What the sort is not

It is not a triage into accept and reject. That decision comes after, per
comment, and it belongs to the author. The sort establishes what kind of
attention each comment needs, which is a different question and a much
easier one to get right early.
