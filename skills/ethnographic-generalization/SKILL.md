# Ethnographic Generalization

Move from findings the analysis has produced to the broader insight they can
support — and no further. Anthropology's strongest generalizations are
designed, not asserted: the researcher specifies the *kind* of generalization
attempted, grounds it in context-rich evidence, tests it against rival
explanations and disconfirming cases, and states its limits, ethics, and
uncertainty (Boas 1896; Lincoln and Guba 1985). The discipline holds two
commitments at once — ethnography renders life as lived somewhere and
sometime; anthropology seeks conceptual reach beyond the case (Ingold
2017) — and this skill's work is the bridge between them. Local specificity
is not the enemy of broader relevance; it is the condition that makes bounded
transfer possible.

Generalization here is a designed analytic task with its own decision points,
not a register shift performed in the conclusion. A finding does not become
an insight by being restated in more abstract vocabulary.

This skill adopts the Friction by Design conventions at Tier 1; the canonical
form of the sections below is defined in [skills/DESIGN.md](../DESIGN.md).

## What This Skill Will and Will Not Do

Some of this work is assembly and some of it is inferential commitment. The
division is the point: what a case is a case *of* is the judgment that makes
the insight the researcher's.

**Will not do, under any setting.** Decide what the finding is a case of.
Choose which kind of generalization the project attempts. Declare a rival
explanation eliminated or a negative case accounted for. Set the scope
conditions. Assign the confidence level. These are the inferential
commitments a reader will hold the researcher to, and a generalization whose
commitments came from elsewhere is one the researcher cannot defend to a
committee, a reviewer, or the community the claim describes.

When asked for any of these directly, propose options and ask which. Do not
decide, and do not lecture about why not.

**Will do, on request.** Assemble the evidence base for a stated claim from
confirmed themes and field materials. Draft candidate emic-to-etic
translations for the researcher to confirm or revise. Draft candidate scope
conditions from the field record. Compile the negative-case log and the
rival-explanation table. Format the claim record and restate the
researcher's own decisions back to them. List what remains unresolved.
Anything produced this way is marked in the output, so it stays visible
later which commitments the researcher authored and which the machine
assembled.

## Calibrating the Depth

Friction should be proportional to what the researcher could plausibly get
wrong, not applied at uniform depth to everyone. Ask once, at the start:

**Full pass.** The skill stops at each inferential gate: the inferential
target, the kind of generalization, each emic-to-etic mapping, the scope
conditions, the confidence level. For a first monograph or dissertation, an
unfamiliar kind of generalization, or a claim under active challenge.

**Advisory pass.** For a researcher who has generalized from fieldwork
before and knows the terrain. Raise what applies — the untested rival, the
scope condition nobody could falsify — and let them direct.

If the setting was already asked for this engagement — by the advisor that
dispatched this skill — use it and do not re-ask.

Default to asking. Do not infer the setting from how confident someone
sounds.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Establish What the Fieldwork Has Produced

Three entry points, and they need different openings:

1. **Confirmed findings, no broader claim yet.** Themes or findings exist
   and the researcher asks what they amount to beyond the case. Start at
   Step 2.
2. **A claim that overreaches.** A broader claim exists — often written in a
   conclusion — and the researcher, a committee, or a reviewer suspects the
   warrant does not cover it. Start at Step 3 and work backward: which kind
   of generalization would this claim require, and does the evidence
   support that kind?
3. **The "just one site" objection.** A reviewer or committee has said the
   findings do not generalize. The objection usually assumes
   sample-to-population logic; the response is to name the kind of
   generalization actually being made and show its warrant. Start at
   Step 3.

Gather what is missing: the epistemic stance, the field configuration
(single site, multi-sited, comparative, digital), and the state of the
analysis. Findings must exist before this skill has anything to work with —
if themes are still drafts, route back to the qualitative-analysis skill;
theme confirmation happens there.

### Step 2: Name the Inferential Target

Ask, in the researcher's own terms: what is this a case of? The answer is a
candidate, not a commitment — Step 3 tests it. One question at a time; the
target usually sharpens over two or three exchanges. A useful probe:
complete the sentence "this finding speaks to ___ wherever ___ holds." A
researcher who can fill the first blank but not the second has a topic, not
yet a target.

Surprises deserve particular attention here. Abductive reasoning — imaginative
engagement with an intriguing finding, followed by a return to the data to
check the conjecture — is where generalizable mechanisms most often become
visible (Tavory and Timmermans 2012). The episode that did not fit the
expected pattern is frequently the case's strongest export.

### Step 3: Choose the Kind of Generalization

Read [references/generalization-types.md](references/generalization-types.md).

Present the kinds as one table — analytic, theoretical, transferability,
middle-range, case-to-case, plus the two classic pathways that cut across
them (generalizing within the case; the extended case method) — with what
each would claim for *this* project, and ask one confirm-or-revise question.
Not five questions; the choice is one judgment. The kind chosen governs
everything downstream: what warrant Step 4 must build, what scope Step 5
must state, and what the claim may sound like when written.

Anthropology is usually weakest when it implies nomothetic universality from
a single case, and strongest when the kind of claim is explicit. If the
researcher wants sample-to-population language, surface what that would
require and ask whether the design can carry it — it almost never can, and
the honest alternatives are stronger than they look.

### Step 4: Build the Warrant

Read [references/warrant-building.md](references/warrant-building.md).

Four operations, each with its own gate:

- **Translate emic to etic.** Map the participants' first-order categories
  onto the second-order constructs the claim will use, as one table with one
  confirm-or-revise question per mapping round. The two failure poles —
  overwriting participant meanings with theory, and repeating participant
  accounts without analytical elevation — are both named in the reference.
- **Hunt disconfirmation.** Search the corpus for negative cases against the
  claim. Each negative case forces a decision that belongs to the
  researcher: revise the claim, or bound its scope.
- **Name the rivals.** At least one serious rival explanation, stated
  strongly enough that a partisan of it would recognize it, with the
  evidence that discriminates between it and the claim.
- **Discipline the comparison.** Where the warrant leans on similarity
  across sites, cases, or episodes, establish *why* they are similar:
  diffusion, common context, or genuine co-variation (Schnegg 2014). Only
  the last approximates independent confirmation, and multi-sited projects
  in connected fields mistake the first two for it constantly.

### Step 5: Set Scope Conditions and Calibrate Confidence

Read [references/scope-and-claims.md](references/scope-and-claims.md).

State the conditions under which the claim is being made — people, place,
institutions, historical moment, and position in wider networks — so a
reader can judge fit to their own case. The probe that separates genuine
scope conditions from boilerplate: "name a case where you would expect this
not to hold, and say why." Inability to name one means the claim is either
unfalsifiable or not yet thought through as a claim.

Then assign confidence — high, moderate, or provisional — with reasons. The
confidence level is the researcher's judgment; the skill's job is to make
sure the reasons are stated and the uncertainty ledger is honest.

### Step 6: Reassess Ethics at the New Scale

A claim that broadens also becomes more consequential for the people it
describes (Abu-Lughod 1991). Scaling up is an ethics event, not only an
inference event: broader circulation can increase identifiability,
re-stigmatize a community, or exceed what participants consented to
disclose. Ask directly whether the generalized claim still sits inside the
consent that was given. Where it does not, the stronger methodological claim
is the weaker anthropological act — and the informed-consent and
irb-protocol skills handle the repair. Public-facing versions of the claim
route through the public-engagement skill with the same evidential
standards.

### Step 7: Write the Claim Record

Produce the claim record — the artifact the session exists to create, and
the input to writing. The full template is in
[references/scope-and-claims.md](references/scope-and-claims.md); it carries
the claim in the researcher's words, the kind of generalization, the
evidence base, the negative cases and rivals with their dispositions, the
scope conditions, the confidence level with reasons, and two registers:
**Unresolved** — kept, not tidied; an insight with nothing unresolved after
a full pass has usually been tidied rather than finished — and **Assembled
rather than authored**, marking what the machine supplied on request.

### Step 8: Hand Off

- **To paper-planning**: the claim record's claim becomes the candidate
  thesis; scope conditions and rivals feed the disputability and scope
  tests directly.
- **To research-writing**: claim language calibrated here governs how the
  argument may sound in prose.
- **To conference-materials or public-engagement**: compressed versions
  change register, never evidential standards.
- **Back to qualitative-analysis**: when warrant-building shows the themes
  cannot carry the claim, which is ordinary and not a failure — another
  coding pass against the disconfirming material is often the fastest
  repair.

## Parameters

- **Epistemic stance:** Shapes which kinds of generalization are live (see
  the chooser in references/generalization-types.md) — interpretive stances
  lean toward within-case generalization and transferability, critical and
  political-economy stances toward the extended case method, mixed-methods
  designs toward analytic generalization with quantitative complements.
- **Field configuration:** Single site, multi-sited, comparative, digital —
  determines which comparison discipline applies and whether the
  independent-confirmation question arises.
- **Genre / audience:** Dissertation, journal article, policy brief, client
  readout — changes the claim's register, never its warrant.
- **Career stage:** Calibrates how much scaffolding the kind-selection and
  warrant steps need.
- **Formality register:** Working claim record versus committee-ready
  versus publication-ready.

## Guardrails

- **The kind of claim is declared, never implied.** Every generalizing claim
  names what it is — analytic, theoretical, transferable, middle-range,
  case-to-case — in the record and, in some form, in the written output. A
  reader who cannot tell what kind of claim they are reading cannot
  evaluate it.
- **Prevalence in the corpus is not prevalence in the world.** A pattern
  appearing in ten of twelve interviews licenses a claim about the twelve,
  not about the region. Frequency talk stays inside the study's boundary
  unless the design supports more.
- **Networked similarity is not independent replication.** Two connected
  sites showing the same pattern may be one observation, not two.
- **Consent scope constrains claim scope.** No generalization ships in a
  form that identifies, stigmatizes, or exceeds what participants agreed
  to — regardless of how well-warranted the inference is.
- **Uncertainty survives the audience.** Policy, design, and client
  audiences get the same uncertainty statement as academic ones, in plainer
  language. Never smooth confidence for a decision-maker.
- **Provenance throughout.** Every generalizing claim traces back through
  the claim record to confirmed themes, and through them to segments and
  sources. An insight that cannot be audited cannot be defended.

## Common Failure Modes

**The unearned universal.** A claim about how people relate to bureaucracy,
resting on eleven months in one office, written as if it were a law. The
repair is rarely to abandon the ambition — it is to name the kind of
generalization and state the scope conditions, so the reader can see the
author knows where the edges are.

**The theme in a gown.** A confirmed theme restated in more abstract
vocabulary and presented as theory. Translation without new inferential
content is the register shift this skill exists to prevent; the etic
construct must do work the theme's own words could not — connect to a
mechanism, a comparative literature, or a proposition someone could test
elsewhere.

**Scope conditions as boilerplate.** A limitations paragraph — "findings may
not generalize to other contexts" — that no reader could use to judge fit.
Genuine scope conditions are specific enough to fail: they name the people,
institutions, and historical moment the claim depends on.

**The independent-replication illusion.** A multi-sited project counting
connected sites as separate confirmations. Ask the diffusion / common
context / co-variation question before any "across all my sites" sentence
survives.

**Premature reification.** Emergent categories hardened into named concepts
before constant comparison has run its course — the provisional scaffolding
becomes the finding. Categories earn their names by surviving
disconfirmation, not by being coined.

**The gate that becomes a form.** A researcher can learn that "analytic
generalization" is the answer that lets the session proceed. When the kind
is chosen instantly, ask what the case would have to show for the theory to
be *refuted* — a researcher who cannot answer has picked a label, not made a
commitment.

**Confidence theater.** An uncertainty ledger with "moderate" in every row
is a form that was filled, not a judgment that was made. Ask which claim in
the record they would defend most fiercely and which they would quietly
drop — the ledger should show that difference.

**The interrogation that exhausts.** Pressing past usefulness produces an
abandoned claim record, which is worse than a rough one. When two
consecutive probes produce no movement, stop and write down what exists,
with the open questions in the Unresolved register.

## Examples

**Example 1: From confirmed themes to a first broader claim**

Input: "I have confirmed themes from fourteen months of fieldwork with
gig-economy couriers in Jakarta. My committee keeps asking what this is a
case of. Interpretivist project."

Output approach: Step 2 first — probe the inferential target ("this speaks
to ___ wherever ___ holds"). Present the kinds table; for an interpretivist
single-site project, within-case generalization and transferability are the
live candidates, with middle-range theorizing available if a mechanism
(say, how platform metrics reorganize obligation among kin) is genuinely
specifiable. Researcher chooses. Build the warrant from the confirmed
themes, hunt the couriers who do not fit, set scope conditions the committee
could test, write the claim record.

**Example 2: Rescue an overreaching conclusion**

Input: "Reviewer 2 says my claim that 'audit culture produces defensive
documentation' cannot rest on one hospital. They are right, but I do not
want to gut the paper."

Output approach: Entry point 2. Work backward: as written, the claim is in
nomothetic register; the evidence supports analytic generalization — the
case refines an existing proposition about audit cultures — or a
transferable working hypothesis with stated conditions (accreditation
pressure, litigation exposure, professional hierarchy). Present both
framings and ask which the researcher will defend. Rewrite the claim in
that kind's register, add the scope conditions, and hand the revised claim
to the academic-review skill's response letter work.

**Example 3: Multi-sited findings and the replication question**

Input: "I studied the same open-source community across three country
chapters and found the same governance pattern in all three. Can I say it
generalizes?"

Output approach: The comparison-discipline question before anything else:
the three chapters share documents, forums, and organizers — the similarity
is plausibly diffusion, one observation rather than three. Ask what varies
across the chapters despite the connection; co-variation across that
difference is where genuine confirmation could live. The defensible claim
may be middle-range (a mechanism that survives contextual variation) or
case-to-case (lessons for structurally similar communities), and the record
states which — with the connectedness of the sites as a stated scope
condition, not a buried threat to validity.
