# Kinds of Generalization

The single most useful move in this work is to disaggregate "generalization."
Most objections to ethnographic generalization — and most overreaching
responses to those objections — assume there is one kind, the
sample-to-population kind, and that ethnography either achieves it or fails.
Ethnography almost never achieves it and almost never needs it. The
discipline's actual repertoire is below. The researcher chooses one; the
choice governs the warrant, the scope statement, and the register of the
written claim.

## The Five Kinds

| Kind | Core question | What travels | Typical warrant in anthropology | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Analytic generalization** | Does this case refine, extend, or challenge a theory? | A theoretical proposition or mechanism | Pattern matching against the theory's predictions; explanation building; comparison with rivals | Treating one case as direct population evidence |
| **Theoretical (substantive) generalization** | Can emergent categories become a substantive theory? | Categories and their interrelations | Constant comparison, theoretical sampling, saturation of categories | Reifying provisional categories too early |
| **Transferability** | Under what conditions might this hold elsewhere? | A bounded working hypothesis | Thick description dense enough that a *reader* can judge fit between sending and receiving contexts | Vague context reporting that prevents any fit assessment |
| **Middle-range theory** | What mid-level mechanism links local findings to broader processes? | An empirically revisable proposition about a mechanism | Comparative cases, process tracing, mechanism claims close to observable behavior | Drifting into banal description or grand theory |
| **Case-to-case transfer** | Does another case resemble this one closely enough to learn from it? | Practical lessons | Demonstrated similarity across people, setting, time, institutions | Assuming similarity without demonstrating it |

Sources for the disaggregation: Firestone (1993) distinguishes
sample-to-population extrapolation, analytic generalization, and
case-to-case transfer, and notes qualitative work relies on the latter two.
Lincoln and Guba (1985) replace "generalizability" with transferability:
naturalistic inquiry yields working hypotheses whose movement to another
setting depends on contextual similarity, and the burden of judging that
similarity lies partly with the reader — which is why thick description is
methodological infrastructure, not ornament. Merton (1949) defines
middle-range theory as intermediate between detailed description and grand
systems too remote from observed behavior. Substantive theory-building is
the grounded-theory tradition (Glaser and Strauss 1967).

Analytic and theoretical generalization are often intertwined but not
identical. Analytic generalization *starts from* an explicit relation to
existing theory — the case supports, qualifies, or contradicts a
proposition. Theoretical generalization *builds toward* theory from
emergent categories. A project can do either; it should know which.

## Two Classic Pathways That Cut Across the Kinds

**Generalizing within the case (Geertz).** The interpretive tradition's
answer: the essential task is not to generalize across cases toward
cross-cultural regularities but to generalize *within* them — to show how a
microscopic social event instantiates and clarifies macro-level structures,
so that small facts speak to large issues because they are made to (Geertz
1973). Validity rests not on the statistical prevalence of the event but on
the clarity with which the localized interpretation illuminates broader
dynamics. "Deep Play" moves from one cockfight to status, honor, and
personhood without ever claiming all gambling works this way. Within-case
generalization typically issues in theoretical generalization or
transferability, and it lives or dies on thick description with explicit
salience — detail without analytic relevance is clutter.

**The extended case method (Manchester School; Burawoy).** The structural
tradition's answer: begin *with* existing macro theory rather than an
assumed blank slate; select the site because observed reality fails to
unfold as the theory predicts; trace how institutional histories, power,
and political economy penetrate the local; and generalize by
*reconstructing the theory* to account for the anomaly (Burawoy 1998). The
product is analytic generalization with an explicitly structural warrant.
The site's value is its anomaly, not its typicality.

The two pathways are not stance-neutral, and the choice between them
usually follows the project's stance rather than preceding it.

## Choosing: A Diagnostic

Present these as one decision, not a questionnaire. The researcher's
answers to "what is this a case of" (Step 2) usually make one row obvious.

| If the finding is best described as... | The natural kind |
|---|---|
| A case that qualifies, extends, or contradicts a named proposition in the literature | Analytic generalization (extended case method if the entry point was the anomaly) |
| A set of emergent categories that interrelate and were not in the literature going in | Theoretical (substantive) generalization |
| A richly contextualized pattern whose value is that readers with similar cases will recognize and test it | Transferability |
| A specifiable mechanism — a process that links conditions to outcomes and could be looked for elsewhere without assuming it operates identically | Middle-range theory |
| Practical lessons for a named class of similar settings (another clinic, another platform, another program) | Case-to-case transfer |

Stance inflects the menu. Interpretive, phenomenological, and hermeneutic
stances sit naturally with within-case generalization and transferability.
Critical, political-economy, and postcolonial stances sit naturally with
the extended case method. Practice theory and STS projects often carry
middle-range mechanisms well. Applied and design-anthropology projects
usually need transferability or case-to-case transfer, because a client's
question is precisely "does this hold for my context." Mixed-methods
designs can support analytic generalization with quantitative complements —
used as additional lenses on the same problem, never as automatic
validators.

Two honesty checks on the choice:

- **The ambition check.** If the researcher reaches for
  sample-to-population language ("couriers in Southeast Asia," "hospitals
  under audit regimes"), ask what the design would have needed — sampling
  for representativeness across the claimed population — and whether it has
  it. Boas's (1896) warning still governs: superficial similarity across
  cases is not evidence of the same process, and comparison earns its force
  only when grounded in historically and contextually specific inquiry.
- **The modesty check.** The opposite failure is real: a researcher trained
  to disclaim everything may bury a genuinely portable mechanism in
  "this is just my site." If the warrant supports middle-range theory,
  transferability language undersells it — and the reader loses a concept
  they could have used.

## What Each Kind Demands Downstream

| Kind | The warrant must show (Step 4) | The scope statement must carry (Step 5) |
|---|---|---|
| Analytic | Pattern match or mismatch against the theory's actual predictions, not a paraphrase of them; rivals considered | Which parts of the theory the case speaks to, and which it cannot |
| Theoretical | Categories saturated; near-cases compared; the interrelations, not just the category list | The substantive area the theory is *of* |
| Transferability | Thick description with explicit salience: what about the context does the pattern depend on | The sending context's parameters, dense enough for a reader's fit judgment |
| Middle-range | The mechanism traced in the data, process by process; at least one comparison across variation | The conditions under which the mechanism is expected to operate, and known suppressors |
| Case-to-case | Similarity demonstrated on the axes that matter, not asserted | The class of receiving cases, named |

## Exemplars

Classic and contemporary ethnographies, read as generalization strategies:

| Ethnography | From finding | To broader insight | Kind most defensible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Malinowski, *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) | Kula exchange practices | Exchange as status-laden social process | Analytic and middle-range |
| Evans-Pritchard, *The Nuer* (1940) | Livelihood and lineage organization | Segmentary, stateless political order | Analytic — and a standing caution: powerful models flatten what they exclude, and later critique traced exactly that |
| Geertz, "Deep Play" (1972) | One Balinese cockfight | Symbolic dramatization of status and personhood | Within-case, issuing in theoretical generalization and transferability |
| Bourgois, *In Search of Respect* (1995) | East Harlem crack economy | Social marginalization and alienated labor under structural inequality | Analytic and case-to-case |
| Scheper-Hughes, *Death Without Weeping* (1993) | Maternal triage under scarcity | Everyday violence and social suffering | Analytic and middle-range |
| Tsing, *Friction* (2005) | Indonesian rainforest encounters | "Friction" — universalizing projects enacted through sticky, contingent connection | Middle-range and theoretical; the model case of moving from a site to a portable concept without erasing the mess that produced it |

The pattern across all six: the generalization is strongest not where the
fieldsite is treated as typical, but where the work specifies the mechanism
or meaning-structure connecting intimate observation to larger process —
and says what sort of abstraction it is making.
