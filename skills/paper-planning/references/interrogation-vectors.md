# Interrogation Vectors

Eight diagnostic angles for pressing on a plan. They are not a sequence to work
through in order. They are a set to draw from when something in the plan feels
unresolved and it is not obvious what is wrong.

Each vector names a distinct way an argument fails, gives the anthropological
form of that failure, and supplies probes. Use one or two per session. Running
all eight on every plan converts a dialogue into an interrogation, which is the
failure mode this whole approach exists to avoid.

## Choosing a Vector From the Symptom

| What the author says or shows | Vector to run |
|---|---|
| A key term keeps shifting meaning across the plan | Clarity |
| The material is asserted to show something it may not show | Accuracy |
| The claim is stated in general terms with no stated limits | Precision |
| A section is present but nothing depends on it | Relevance |
| The plan reports a pattern without saying why the pattern holds | Depth |
| The argument reads as the only possible reading of the material | Breadth |
| The conclusion does not obviously follow from the sections before it | Logic |
| Several claims are present and none is clearly primary | Significance |

---

## Clarity

**The failure.** A term is doing important work in the argument without a stable
meaning. Anthropology is exposed here because the discipline borrows heavily and
the borrowed terms arrive with different meanings from different traditions.
"Agency," "care," "infrastructure," "the state," and "community" are all capable
of shifting sense between the introduction and the analysis without anyone
noticing.

**Probes.**
- "Give me your working definition of that term, in one sentence, without using
  the term."
- "Whose usage are you taking it from, and are you keeping their sense of it?"
- "Where in the plan does this term first appear, and does it mean the same
  thing at its last appearance?"
- "If a reader substituted the ordinary-language meaning, which of your
  sentences would still be true?"

**Repair signal.** If the author needs a paragraph to define the term, the term
may be carrying an argument that should be made explicitly rather than smuggled
in through vocabulary.

---

## Accuracy

**The failure.** The evidence does not establish what it is said to establish.
In ethnographic work this rarely looks like a factual error. It looks like
material recruited as demonstration when it is functioning as illustration: the
scene is consistent with the claim but would be equally consistent with two
other readings.

**Probes.**
- "What would you expect to have observed if this claim were false?"
- "Is this excerpt evidence for the claim, or an example of the claim? They are
  different, and only one persuades a skeptic."
- "How many of your interlocutors does this pattern hold for, and how do you
  know about the ones it does not hold for?"
- "Would someone who was there and disagreed with you describe this scene
  differently, and how?"

---

## Precision

**The failure.** The claim has no stated boundaries, so a reader cannot tell
what it commits the author to. This is the unearned universal in its Phase 3
form: the scope problem survived claim extraction and is now shaping sections.

**Probes.**
- "Under what conditions would you expect this not to hold?"
- "Is this a claim about this community, about communities of this kind, or
  about people generally? The paper should say which."
- "What is the time frame? Does the claim describe a moment, a period, or a
  tendency?"
- "Name the nearest case where the claim would be false."

---

## Relevance

**The failure.** Material is present that nothing depends on. The
straightforward version is the unused background section. The subtle version is
a theoretical apparatus introduced early and never applied, which reviewers
detect quickly and read as unfamiliarity with the tradition being cited.

**Probes.**
- "Point at the later sentence this section makes possible."
- "If a reader skipped this entirely, which of your later claims would they fail
  to follow?"
- "Is this here because the argument needs it, or because it would be strange to
  write about this topic without it?"

---

## Depth

**The failure.** The plan establishes that something is the case without
establishing why. Description of a pattern is presented as explanation of it.
This is the most common gap between a competent paper and a strong one.

**Probes.**
- "What mechanism produces this? Not what happens, but what makes it happen."
- "You have said these two things co-occur. What connects them?"
- "What is the hardest thing for your explanation to account for?"
- "Where does your own account break down, and what do you do at that point?"

---

## Breadth

**The failure.** The argument is presented as the reading of the material rather
than a reading. The author has one analytic and has not tested the material
against another, so the paper cannot say why this reading beats the alternatives.

This vector connects directly to the toolkit's analytical lens registry. The
same material read through a different lens attends to different things, and the
divergence is diagnostic: where two lenses converge, the finding is robust to
framing; where they diverge, the author is committed to a position and should
argue for it rather than assume it. The registry is documented in
[../../DESIGN.md](../../DESIGN.md).

**Probes.**
- "Read your central scene through a different lens. What comes forward that
  your current reading leaves out?"
- "What would a scholar who rejects your theoretical commitments say about this
  material?"
- "Which competing explanation is strongest, and why does yours beat it?"
- "Whose account of this situation are you not representing, and is that
  defensible?"

---

## Logic

**The failure.** The chain of inference has a missing or reversed link. Often
survives to submission because each section is individually sound and the defect
only appears in the joins.

**Probes.**
- "Walk me from your first section to your conclusion in five sentences, each
  following from the last."
- "Does your conclusion follow from your material, or from your framing of your
  material?"
- "You concluded X. Which section established the premise that makes X follow?"
- "Is there a step here where the reader has to take your word for it?"

---

## Significance

**The failure.** Several true claims are present and the paper has not decided
which one it is making. Frequently the interesting subsidiary observation is
better than the stated thesis, and the author has not noticed.

**Probes.**
- "If the paper could keep only one of these claims, which one?"
- "Is this the central problem, or an interesting thing you found along the
  way?"
- "What does the field do differently if you are right?"
- "Which of your claims would a reader most want to argue with? That may be your
  real contribution."

---

## A Note on How Many

The point of the vectors is diagnostic reach, not coverage. An author who has
been asked eight sets of questions about a plan they were reasonably confident
in will usually finish less confident and no clearer, which is the opposite of
the intent.

Pick the vector the symptom points to. Run it until something moves. Stop.
