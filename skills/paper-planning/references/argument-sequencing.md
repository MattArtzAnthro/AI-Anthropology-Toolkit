# Argument Sequencing

Phase 3 of paper planning. The claim exists and the position is established.
What remains is the order: which section comes first, what job each one does,
and whether the sequence forms an argument or merely a sequence.

This is not the same as knowing the conventional section list. Most authors
already know a paper has an introduction, a literature section, methods,
material, and a discussion. Knowing the list does not tell you why the material
section has to come after the theory section in this paper and before it in that
one. That is what this phase settles.

## One Job Per Section

Start by making the author state, for each planned section, the single job it
does in establishing the claim. One sentence, and the sentence must reference
the claim.

The useful form is: *"This section exists so the reader will accept X, which the
argument needs before Y can be made."*

Sections that resist this framing are the ones to interrogate. Three failures
show up repeatedly.

**The section with two jobs.** Usually a literature section that is also doing
theoretical framing, or a material section that is also doing analysis. Not
always wrong, but the author should know it is happening, because two-job
sections are where readers lose the thread. Probe: "Which of those two jobs
could move elsewhere, and what breaks if it does?"

**The section with no job.** It is present because papers have that section. The
context or background section is the usual offender: several pages of regional
history, colonial background, or policy timeline that the argument never draws
on. Probe: "Point at the later moment where this section pays off. If you cannot
find one, what is it doing here?"

**The section whose job is the paper's job.** If a section's stated job is the
claim itself, the argument is concentrated in one place and the rest of the
paper is scaffolding around it. Sometimes correct for a short piece. More often
it means the other sections have not been thought of as argumentative.

## The Necessity Test

For each adjacent pair of sections, ask why the first must precede the second.

A real answer names something the reader acquires in the first that the second
depends on: a definition, a piece of context without which an event is
unreadable, a theoretical distinction the analysis then applies, a doubt the
next section resolves.

A non-answer is any of: it flows better, that is the standard order, it is
chronological, or it builds up. Chronology in particular is worth pressing on,
because fieldwork happened in an order and that order exerts a pull on the
writing that has nothing to do with the argument. The sequence of discovery is
rarely the sequence of persuasion.

Then run the test in reverse: **what would the reader misunderstand if these two
were swapped?** If the answer is nothing, the order is arbitrary, which is
permitted but should be a decision rather than an accident.

## Concept First or Case First

The most consequential ordering decision in an anthropological paper, and it is
a genuine choice rather than a convention.

**Concept first.** Theory, then the case that tests or extends it. The reader
arrives at the material already holding the analytic. Commits the paper to the
concept being the thing at stake: the case is in service of the argument about
the concept. Suits the recontextualization and extension positions. The risk is
that the material reads as illustration, and reviewers say the ethnography is
doing no work.

**Case first.** The material, then the analytic that emerges from it. The reader
encounters the situation before being told how to read it. Commits the paper to
the case being the thing at stake, with theory recruited to make sense of it.
Suits the complication and counter-claim positions. The risk is that the reader
does not know what to attend to, and the analysis reads as arriving late to
rescue a description.

The probe that settles it: **is the concept on trial, or is the case on trial?**
Only one can be, and the order should follow the answer. When an author wants
both, the paper usually has two arguments and needs to shed one.

## Where the Opening Scene Goes

Anthropological papers often open with a scene, and the scene's placement is a
sequencing decision with consequences the author should make deliberately.

An opening scene can do one of three jobs, and it should do only one:

- **Establish stakes.** The reader sees why this matters before being told.
- **Pose the puzzle.** The scene contains something that does not make sense
  under the field's current categories, and the paper resolves it.
- **Ground the abstraction.** The scene gives a concrete referent the argument
  can point back to whenever the theory gets thin.

Probe: "Which of the three is your opening doing?" And then: "Does the scene
return later, and where?" A scene that never returns was decoration. A scene
that returns at the moment the claim is made is doing structural work, and
noting that now tells the author what the closing section has to reach back to.

## Proportion

Have the author allocate approximate word counts across sections, then compare
the allocation against the claim.

The recurring problem in ethnographic writing is that the material section is
sized by how much good material exists rather than by how much the argument
requires. Fifteen pages of extraordinary fieldnotes supporting a claim that
needs four is not generosity toward the reader. It buries the argument and
invites the reviewer to find a different paper in the material than the one
being argued.

The reverse also occurs, more often in theoretically ambitious work: a large
conceptual apparatus resting on two interview excerpts. Probe: "Would a skeptic
say the evidence carries the weight of the claim?"

Useful diagnostic: the section that receives the most words should be the
section doing the most argumentative work. When it is not, ask which the author
would defend if a reviewer demanded the paper lose two thousand words.

## The Second Case Problem

Common in comparative and multi-sited work. The author has material from more
than one site and plans to include all of it.

Adding a second case does one of two things. It strengthens the claim by showing
the argument holds across conditions the reader would expect to matter. Or it
dilutes the paper by halving the depth available to each case while adding a
comparison the argument never uses.

Probe: "What does the second case let you say that the first alone could not?"
If the answer is "it shows the same thing," the second case is redundant and
belongs in a footnote or a different paper. If the answer names a contrast the
argument turns on, it is load-bearing and the sequencing has to make the
contrast visible rather than leaving the reader to construct it.

## Reflexivity Placement

Anthropology requires the author's position to be accounted for, and where that
accounting sits is a structural choice that shapes how it reads.

- **Confined to a methods section**: contained and easy for a reader to skip.
  Reads as disclosure. Appropriate when positionality is a condition of the work
  rather than part of its argument.
- **Woven through**: the author's presence is visible where it mattered. Reads
  as method. Appropriate when the encounter itself is evidence.
- **In the opening**: positions the whole paper. Reads as a claim about how
  knowledge was made. The strongest and the riskiest, because it commits the
  paper to sustaining that register.

Probe: "Is your position a condition of the research or part of the argument?"
The answer determines the placement, and the placement should not be inherited
from whatever the author's last paper did.

## Reverse Outlining as a Check

When a rough draft or extensive notes already exist, this is the most reliable
verification available for a planned sequence.

Have the author write one sentence per existing section, stating what that
section actually establishes. Not what it is about. What it establishes. Then
read the sentences in order, alone, without the paper.

Three things become visible immediately and are difficult to see any other way:
whether the sentences form a chain in which each depends on the one before,
where a step is missing, and where two sentences say the same thing. Any of the
three is a sequencing defect that would otherwise be found by a reviewer.

## Transitions as Diagnostics

At each major boundary, ask what the reader has to carry across it.

A transition that cannot be stated in a sentence usually marks a real
discontinuity in the argument rather than a stylistic gap. The pivot from a
theoretical section to a material section is where this shows up most often: the
author has framed the material with concepts that the analysis then does not
use. Being unable to write the bridge is the signal, and the repair is upstream
in the argument rather than in the sentence.
