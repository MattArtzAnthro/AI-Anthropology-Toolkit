# Claim Extraction and the Rhetorical Position

Phase 1 of paper planning. The work here is to move from having material to
having an argument, and then to establish what that argument is an argument
*against*.

## The Distinction That Organizes This Phase

There are two things a scholar can do with what they know. The first is to
report it: set down what was observed, what the sources say, what happened at
the site, organized by whatever order the material itself suggests. The second
is to argue: subordinate all of that material to a claim it is being used to
establish, discarding whatever does not serve the claim however interesting it
may be.

Reporting feels productive because the page fills. It is also the single most
common reason a paper with excellent material fails review. The reviewer's
complaint arrives in a recognizable form: "rich description, but I am not sure
what the argument is," or "the case is compelling but underdetermined by the
theory."

In anthropology this failure has a characteristic shape. Ethnographic writing
rewards descriptive density, so the descriptive mode is both a disciplinary
virtue and the trap. A paper can be beautifully observed, ethically careful,
theoretically literate, and still not argue anything. The question that
separates the two modes is not "is this well described?" It is "what would have
to be false for this paper to be wrong?"

A claim that cannot be wrong is not a claim.

## The Six-Month Test

Ask the author: **six months after reading this, what is the one sentence your
reader still remembers?**

Then hold the answer against these failures. Each substitutes something else
for the claim, and each is common enough to have its own diagnostic.

| Substitution | Sounds like | Why it is not a claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Site for claim** | "This is a paper about a fishing cooperative in Kerala." | Names where the argument happens, not what it argues. Every paper about that site would have the same thesis. |
| **Topic for claim** | "This paper is about care work and migration." | Names a subject area. A subject area cannot be disputed. |
| **Method for claim** | "This is a multi-sited ethnography of supply chains." | Names how the knowing was done. Method is a warrant for a claim, not the claim. |
| **Theorist for claim** | "This is a Foucauldian reading of the clinic." | Names the apparatus. Applying a framework correctly is not by itself a contribution. |
| **Finding for claim** | "I found that workers resist in indirect ways." | Reports a pattern without saying what the pattern establishes or what it revises. |
| **Stance for claim** | "This paper takes seriously the perspectives of my interlocutors." | Names a commitment. A commitment is a condition of good work, not its argument. |

When the author lands on one of these, do not correct it and do not supply the
missing claim. Ask what the substitution is standing in for. "You have told me
where and how. What does the where and how let you say that could not be said
without them?"

## Working the Claim Into Shape

Once a candidate claim exists, three properties make it usable. Probe them in
this order, because each depends on the one before it.

**Disputability.** Who disagrees, and are they real? A claim nobody holds the
opposite of is a description wearing an argument's clothes. Useful probe: state
the opposite of the claim out loud. If the opposite is obviously absurd, the
claim is too weak to be worth making. If the opposite is what most of the field
currently believes, the claim may be strong enough that the paper needs more
evidence than it has.

**Scope.** Under what conditions does the claim fail? Anthropology is unusually
exposed here because the empirical base is often one site, one community, or a
handful of interlocutors, while the theoretical ambition is general. The failure
mode is the unearned universal: a claim about how people relate to bureaucracy
resting on eleven months in one office. The repair is rarely to abandon the
ambition. It is to state the conditions under which the claim is being made, so
the reader can see the author knows where the edges are.

Probe: "Name a case where you would expect this not to hold, and say why."
Inability to name one is diagnostic. It usually means the claim is either
unfalsifiable or has not been thought through as a claim at all.

**Load-bearing premises.** What must a reader already accept before the argument
can even begin? Every paper has these, and unstated ones are where hostile
reviews start. In anthropology they are often ontological or political rather
than empirical: that the category under study is coherent, that the
interlocutors' account is evidence of something beyond itself, that the
comparison being drawn is legitimate.

Probe: "What would a reader have to grant you in the first three pages for the
rest to work?" Anything named here either needs establishing in the paper or
needs conceding as a limit.

## Establishing the Position

A claim on its own does not tell a reader why the paper needed writing. That
requires positioning the claim against the existing conversation, and this is
where most planning stalls.

The generic version of this move assumes a hole in the literature waiting to be
filled. That model travels badly into anthropology, where "nobody has studied
this" is frequently the weakest available position and sometimes an admission
that the topic was not worth studying. Anthropology's contributions are more
often reframings than fillings.

Six positions are available. They are not equally strong, and the strongest for
a given paper depends on the material.

**1. The unexamined case.** Nobody has looked here.

*Credible when* the site or population is genuinely consequential and its absence
from the record has itself been consequential. *Weak when* it is the only thing
the paper can say for itself. Novelty of site is not novelty of argument. Probe:
"If someone had already worked at a comparable site, would your argument change,
or would only your examples change?" If only the examples, this is not the
position.

**2. The counter-claim.** The settled reading is wrong.

*Credible when* the author can state the settled reading fairly, in the terms its
holders would recognize, and then show where it breaks. *Weak when* the settled
reading is a straw version nobody actually defends. Probe: "Name the strongest
version of the position you are arguing against, and name who holds it."

**3. The recontextualization.** A concept that works elsewhere travels badly
here, and the failure is informative about the concept.

*Credible when* the concept's failure reveals something about its hidden
assumptions rather than merely reporting a poor fit. This is one of
anthropology's most durable moves. *Weak when* it reduces to "my case is
different," which is true of every case. Probe: "What does the concept turn out
to have been assuming, that your site makes visible?"

**4. The complication.** The categories the literature uses cannot hold what is
actually going on.

*Credible when* the author can name the specific categorical seam that fails and
show what falls through it. *Weak when* "it is more complicated than that" is
the whole argument. Complexity is the null hypothesis in ethnography and
asserting it is not a contribution. Probe: "Which distinction breaks, and what
specifically falls between its terms?"

**5. The extension.** A tradition developed for one domain is carried into
another.

*Credible when* the carrying is not automatic, so that the extension itself
requires argument and the tradition is changed by the move. *Weak when* it is
application rather than extension, which is coursework. Probe: "What does the
tradition have to give up to work here?"

**6. The reframing.** The question the field has been asking is the wrong
question.

*Credible when* the author can show why the wrong question kept producing a
recognizable kind of unsatisfying answer. The most ambitious position and the
hardest to sustain. *Weak when* it is asserted rather than demonstrated. Probe:
"What has the field's current question been unable to see, and what evidence is
there that it could not see it?"

## Occupying the Position

The last move is to state what the paper does about the position it has
established. Three things must be present, and they are usually the easiest part
of the phase once the first two moves are settled.

- **What the paper claims**, stated once, plainly, in a form the author is
  willing to be wrong about.
- **How the material bears on it.** Not what the material is, but what it is
  evidence *for*. In anthropology this is the link that most often goes
  unarticulated, because the connection feels self-evident to someone who was
  there.
- **What the reader is asked to do with it.** What follows for the field if the
  claim holds.

## The Buried Thesis

A recurring diagnostic worth running at the end of Phase 1. Ask the author to
point at where the claim currently appears in any existing draft or notes. If
the honest answer is the conclusion, or the third paragraph of the discussion,
the paper has been written as a report and the argument was discovered during
the writing.

This is not a failure. It is the ordinary way arguments get found. But the paper
now has to be replanned around the claim rather than lightly edited, and authors
consistently underestimate how much restructuring that takes. Naming it here
saves a revision cycle later.
