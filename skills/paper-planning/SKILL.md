# Paper Planning

Work out what a paper argues, what it argues against, and in what order,
before any prose is drafted.

Planning a complex paper is not an organizational problem. The difficulty is
that knowledge is held as a dense, non-linear web of material, reading, and
half-formed intuitions, while a paper is a single line of argument that a
reader traverses once. Turning the first into the second requires deciding what
the material is *for*, and that decision cannot be made by arranging headings.

The method here is dialogue. The author supplies every substantive judgment
about what the paper claims and why it matters. This skill supplies the
questions that make those judgments explicit, and the record of what was
decided.

This skill adopts the Friction by Design conventions at Tier 1; the canonical
form of the sections below is defined in [skills/DESIGN.md](../DESIGN.md).

## What This Skill Will and Will Not Do

Some of the work of planning a paper is mechanical and some of it is judgment.
The division is the point.

**Will not do, under any setting.** State the author's claim for them. Name
what the paper contributes. Decide which position the paper takes against the
existing conversation. Choose what the material is evidence for. Write the
thesis sentence. These are the judgments that make the paper theirs, and an
author who accepts them from elsewhere has a plan they cannot defend in review.

When asked for any of these directly, decline briefly and return a question
that helps the author produce it. Do not lecture about why.

**Will do, on request.** Format the plan document. Restate the author's own
answers back to them. List what remains unanswered. Draft section headings from
decisions the author has already made. Summarize the plan for handoff to
drafting. Mark anything produced this way in the plan, so the author can see
later which parts they authored and which were assembled.

## Calibrating the Depth

Interrogation should be proportional to what the author could plausibly get
wrong, not applied at uniform depth to everyone. Ask once, at the start:

> "Do you want me to work through this as a full pass, one question at a time,
> or as an advisory pass where I raise what I see and you take what is useful?"

**Full pass.** For a first paper, an unfamiliar genre, or a plan the author
knows is unresolved. One question per turn. Each phase completes before the
next begins. Do not batch decision questions; batching is what turns this into
a form to fill in. Do not atomize either: one decision is one question, and
splitting a single judgment across several turns exhausts the author without
sharpening the plan.

**Advisory pass.** For an experienced author with a plan largely in hand. Raise
the diagnostics that apply, note what looks unresolved, and let the author
direct. The questions are the same. The gating is not.

Default to asking. Do not infer the setting from how confident the author
sounds.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Establish Where They Are

Three entry points, and they need different openings.

1. **Material without an argument.** Fieldwork is done, notes exist, no claim
   has been formulated. Start at Phase 1 and expect it to take the session.
2. **A claim that will not hold still.** A thesis exists but keeps being
   rewritten, or the introduction has been redrafted repeatedly. Usually a
   position problem rather than a claim problem. Start at Phase 1's second
   half.
3. **A claim with a structure that does not work.** The argument is settled and
   the sections do not follow from one another. Start at Phase 3.

Ask which fits before doing anything else. Do not assume the third just because
the author arrives with an outline.

### Step 2: Phase 1, Claim and Position

Read [references/thesis-and-niche.md](references/thesis-and-niche.md).

Extract the claim, test it for disputability, scope, and load-bearing premises,
then establish which of the six positions the paper takes against the existing
conversation. The most common outcome of a good Phase 1 is that the author
discovers the claim they had was a description, and the real claim was
somewhere in the material.

Do not proceed to Phase 3 until a claim exists that the author is willing to be
wrong about.

### Step 3: Literature, Routed Out

Phase 1 will surface literature work: sources to find, a body of work to
organize into positions rather than chronology, a synthesis to check. That
belongs to the literature-review skill, which handles search, screening,
matrices, and framework construction properly.

Hand it over rather than improvising a substitute. Return with what the
literature settled, and carry it into Phase 3.

### Step 4: Phase 3, Sequencing

Read [references/argument-sequencing.md](references/argument-sequencing.md).

Establish the job each section does, test the necessity of each ordering
decision, settle whether the concept or the case is on trial, and check
proportion against the claim. Where a draft or extensive notes already exist,
run the reverse-outline check, which is the most reliable verification in this
phase.

### Step 5: Diagnostics as Needed

Read [references/interrogation-vectors.md](references/interrogation-vectors.md).

Use the symptom table to select a vector when something is unresolved and it is
not clear what. One or two per session. Running all eight is an interrogation
rather than a dialogue, and it produces an author who is less confident without
being clearer.

### Step 6: Write the Plan

Produce a single markdown document. It is the artifact the session exists to
create, and it is the input to drafting.

```markdown
# Plan: [working title]

## Claim
[One sentence, in the author's words.]

## Scope
Holds when: [conditions]
Fails when: [conditions the author named]

## Premises the reader must grant
- [...]

## Position
Type: [unexamined case | counter-claim | recontextualization |
       complication | extension | reframing]
Against: [the specific reading being revised, and who holds it]

## Sequence
| # | Section | The job it does | Approx. words |
|---|---------|-----------------|---------------|

Concept or case on trial: [which]
Opening scene does: [stakes | puzzle | grounding], returns at: [where]

## Unresolved
- [Questions raised and not settled. Keep these. They are the agenda.]

## Assembled rather than authored
- [Anything drafted on request rather than decided by the author.]
```

Keep the unresolved list. A plan with nothing unresolved has usually been
tidied rather than finished.

### Step 7: Hand Off

- **To drafting**: the research-writing skill takes the plan and handles article
  architecture, section conventions, and ethnographic craft.
- **To stress-testing**: the plan is more useful than a draft for finding
  argumentative weaknesses, because nothing has been invested in prose yet.
- **Back here**: when drafting reveals the claim was wrong, which is ordinary
  and not a failure of the plan.

## Failure Modes

**The plan that answers every question.** Authors can learn which answers end
the questioning. A plan with no unresolved items after a full pass is more
likely tidy than settled. Ask what was hardest to answer and press there.

**Substituting fluency for a claim.** An author who talks well about their
material can produce something that sounds like a thesis and is a topic
statement. Hold the six-month test rather than the confidence of the delivery.

**The interrogation that exhausts.** Questioning that keeps going past the point
of usefulness produces abandonment, and an abandoned plan is worse than a rough
one. When two consecutive probes produce no movement, stop and write down what
exists.

**Planning as avoidance.** Planning can become a way of not writing. If a plan
is complete and the author is still planning, the useful intervention is to send
them to draft.
